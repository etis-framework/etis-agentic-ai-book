# Part V — Reliability, Security and Recovery

## When correct boundaries meet partial failure

Parts I–III established the distinction between an advisory recommendation, an independently trusted authorization decision, durable operational intent, and authoritative business truth. Part IV exercised those distinctions in six executable checkpoints. **Part V asks whether the same controls still hold when components crash, requests are duplicated, observers disagree, credentials are abused, and recovery happens under pressure.** A correct happy path is not enough: the hardest failures happen between a durable local decision and a consequential remote effect.

The running example stays fixed. CASE-204 is the retailer's replacement complaint; the possible refund is **$500** and the authorized operation is **RF-1042**. The agent-reasoning responsibility (E3) may produce an action proposal through the advisory action-proposal contract (C04), but that proposal cannot authorize a refund. Trusted execution and authority (E5) independently evaluates transaction-specific financial authority through the authorization-decision contract (C05). Durable operational state (E7) records the local operation history through the operational-state contract (C06). Enterprise integration (E6) carries the controlled provider request through the provider-dispatch contract (C07). Authoritative business systems (E8) provide provider observations through the reconciliation contract (C08). Coordination and ownership (E2) manages work through the work-assignment contract (C02) without inventing payment authority. Admission and identity (E1), context retrieval and provenance (E4), and governance and assurance (E9) remain essential. The nine E-domains are logical responsibilities, not a requirement to deploy nine separate services or obtain nine independent failure domains. Logical ownership, trusted enforcement, and physical failure isolation are separate engineering decisions.

Chapter 28 develops the mental model for distributed failures: where responsibility lives, which records can disagree, why `UNKNOWN` is not `FAILED`, and how recovery proceeds without manufacturing a second consequential operation. Chapter 29 tests those same boundaries under adversarial pressure, including a compromised investigator with a genuine E1 token but no separate financial authority; the actual Build 3 E5 process denies its refund attempt. Chapter 30 implements operational metrics, read-only incident classification, escalation and evidence-qualified restoration, without creating a new payment route. Chapter 31 assembles the exact executable evidence, falsifiers, scope exclusions and accountable release decisions needed to justify—or refuse—production adoption. The sequence is deliberate: a system must first define what may be known and done after failure before it can be defended operationally or certified credibly.

**Reader's rule for this part:** a replacement worker may resume investigation but must not manufacture a second consequential operation. The educational Build 5 lease-and-epoch logic is fenced in coordination and ownership (E2); it is **not** enforced inside trusted execution and authority (E5). The provider is simulated, and the runnable examples are teaching checkpoints rather than a production payment platform. Design A (deterministic), Design B (bounded advice plus independent review, the conditional initial production candidate), and Design C (optional bounded investigation) remain alternatives.

For each failure in this part, track the competing histories explicitly: what the worker believed, what coordination and ownership (E2) allowed, what durable operational state (E7) recorded, what authoritative business systems (E8) could attest, what trusted execution and authority (E5) approved, and which accountable human resolves the remainder. That discipline is the bridge from distributed engineering to governance and operations in Part VI.
