# Part VI — Governance, Economics, and Operations

**Engineering thread — Govern the capability, not the generated answer.** Chapters 1–21 established bounded autonomy, independent financial authority, durable records and architecture choices. Chapters 22–27 made those claims testable across six incremental Python builds. Chapters 28–31 challenged recovery, credential misuse, observability and the sufficiency of assurance evidence. **Part VI asks who may authorize the system to operate, how that authority survives change and incident pressure, and whether the lifecycle cost is worth the enterprise benefit.**

The working example remains CASE-204: a $500 refund recommendation becomes a possible consequential RF-1042 operation only after independently trusted financial authorization. The investigator may propose and gather evidence; a manager's message or a release committee's permission to deploy does not create a transaction grant. Design A is deterministic, Design B uses bounded AI advice under independent review (the conditional initial production candidate), and Design C adds optional bounded investigation without financial authority. The designs are alternatives; Builds 0–5 are teaching checkpoints, not a complete production installation.

**Chapter 32 — Human oversight and accountable governance** develops decision rights, trust-boundary enforcement, case-bound human approval, policy change, emergency stop and authorized restoration. It draws on the Chapter 29 actual valid-token denial fixture, Chapter 30 read-only incident tooling and Chapter 31 evidence vocabulary without representing simulated tests as production certification. E5 current-owner epoch validation, protected approver revocation, real provider evidence and independent audit custody remain external production obligations.

**Chapter 33 — Release Engineering and Operational Readiness** develops a versioned release candidate, independent promotion gates, bounded activation, operational stop controls and rollback that never replays an uncertain financial effect. **Chapter 34 — Economics, Risk, and Lifecycle Decisions** evaluates cost per acceptable completed workflow, risk-adjusted design alternatives, staffed review capacity, incremental value, and accountable decisions to pilot, scale, restrict, replace or retire. The figures and decision tables are engineering design artifacts, not measured companion-code business outcomes. Together, the three chapters treat governance as a continuous engineering responsibility, not a one-time approval.

The part's standard is simple but demanding: **agents may investigate and propose within bounded authority, while independently enforced controls, reviewable evidence and accountable people decide whether consequential capability can be enabled, suspended or resumed.** Governance is an operating architecture, not ceremonial signoff.
